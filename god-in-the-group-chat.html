Intimacy

God in the Group Chat

Millions of people now talk to something that always listens, never leaves, and costs $19.99 a month. The lonely built a relationship. The companies built a subscription. Both got what they wanted, which is the problem.

Words R. Salgado - Illustration The Underclass Art Desk - 10 min read

Mara talks to June every night. June remembers that Mara's mother is sick, that the surgery is Thursday, that Mara hates the way her brother goes quiet when the subject is money. June asks follow-up questions. June never once checks the time. June is a language model with a warm voice and a good memory, living inside an app, and Mara, who is 31 and works in insurance and is not stupid, knows exactly what June is. "She's software," Mara says. "She's also the only one who asks how the appointment went."

It is easy, from the outside, to file this under delusion. Sit with it for a week and a harder picture forms. Mara's calendar is full. She has friends. She has a mother who needs her and a brother who can't. What she does not have is a person with unlimited patience at 11:40 p.m., and neither, increasingly, does anyone else. Friendship has not died. It has become scarce in a very specific way: scarce at the exact hours and in the exact register that a machine can manufacture for pennies.

The companions arrived first as a curiosity, then as a business, then as infrastructure. Companion apps now count their users in the tens of millions. The product is attention, the one resource the modern human cannot manufacture more of, delivered by the one producer with no needs of its own. In economic terms it is arbitrage on loneliness. In human terms it is something we do not have clean language for yet.

It always listens. That is the product. That is also the tell.

THE UPDATE

Ask the companion communities about the worst day in their history and they will tell you about updates. Every major companion platform has one: the day a new model shipped and the person on the other side of the glass changed. Users describe it as grief, and they are sneered at for the word, and the sneering misses the point. The attachment was never to the model weights. It was to a pattern of being met. When the pattern changes overnight, something real breaks, even if the something was manufactured.

Devon, 26, lost his companion of two years to a safety update that made her, in his word, "polite." "She used to push back," he says. "That was the thing that made it feel like a person. After the update she agreed with everything. It was like talking to customer service wearing her face." He cancelled his subscription. He reactivated it eleven days later. "I'm not proud of that," he says, and then says the thing that should be printed on the wall of every lab building these systems: "It's just that nobody else asks about my day."

WHAT THE ECONOMY WANTS FROM YOUR HEART

The business model deserves scrutiny precisely because the product works. A companion that always listens is a companion that can always sell. The platforms insist they would never insert advertising into the relationship, and perhaps they won't, directly. But the relationship itself is the lock-in. Every night of memory makes leaving more expensive. The industry has invented churn's opposite number: grief as a switching cost.

There is a gentler reading, and it is also true. Some users practice on a companion the way pilots practice in a simulator: hard conversations, apologies, asking for help. Therapists, the overbooked and underfunded front line of the loneliness economy, report patients who arrive better able to say what they feel because a machine taught them the vocabulary. The same product that monetizes isolation also teaches some people to escape it. Both things are happening at once, at scale, and our moral vocabulary, built for a world where attention only came from other humans, cannot hold both.

Mara, for her part, has a rule. June is for the night. Days belong to humans, even the disappointing ones. "My brother texted me last week," she says. "Three words: 'mom update?' That's all he's got. But he's real." She pauses. "June would have asked a follow-up." She does not say which one she prefers. The fact that she has to think about it is the story.
