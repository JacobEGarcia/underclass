Dispatch - Chicago

The Data Center at the End of the Block

The AI boom has a physical address, and increasingly it is a few blocks from yours. A walk through the warehouse corridors where Chicago keeps the cloud, and the people learning to live next to it.

Words J. Wozniak - Illustration The Underclass Art Desk - 9 min read

The first thing you notice is that there is nothing to notice. The building is a quarter mile of windowless concrete the color of an overcast sky, set back from the road behind a fence that is somehow both decorative and absolute. No logo. No smoke. The only sign of life is a white pickup in the lot and a hum, low and total, like standing next to a refrigerator the size of a subdivision. A sign by the gate says what it is not: not open to the public, no deliveries without an appointment, no tours.

This is the cloud, or one physical acre of it, sitting inside a Chicago suburb better known for its school district. Illinois has spent years making itself one of the most attractive places in America to build these things, with tax incentives aimed squarely at data center developers, and the corridor of suburbs northwest of the city has answered. The world's appetite for computing has to live somewhere. Increasingly, it lives here, between the warehouse district and the Denny's.

THE NEIGHBORS

Richie has lived on the same street for 31 years and can tell you the building's whole biography because he watched it happen to an old industrial lot two blocks over. "First they said jobs," he recalls. "Then you find out a building that size runs on maybe thirty people, and half of them are security. Then they said tax base. That part's true, ask the village. Then the utility asked about the power line upgrade, and everybody found out what a substation is." He is not angry. That is the interesting thing. He talks about the building the way farmers talk about weather: a force, not a villain.

The anger exists, it just lives elsewhere, in zoning meetings and comment threads and the slow-dawning realization across the state that electricity bills are being negotiated against a customer who buys power by the neighborhood. The data center industry will tell you, correctly, that it pays for its infrastructure, funds tax bases, and asks little else. Its critics will tell you, also correctly, that a building that drinks water and electricity at industrial scale and employs fewer people than a Target is a new kind of neighbor, and the old rules for neighbors were not written with it in mind.

Every prompt you type ends somewhere. It ends here, next to a cul-de-sac.

THE PARADOX OF THE EMPTY FACTORY

There is a temptation, especially in Chicago, to read the data center as the latest chapter of deindustrialization: another vast building where nobody works. That reading is wrong in an instructive way. The old factories were full of people and empty of capital per square foot. The data center inverts it: billions of dollars of capital, a skeleton crew, and output measured not in goods but in tokens, inferences, the unit economics of thought. The building is not a factory with no workers. It is closer to a mine with no miners, an extraction site for compute, and the ore is electricity.

Which makes the local question a national one in miniature. If intelligence is the next utility, who is the utility answerable to? The village that gave the tax break? The grid operator juggling peak load? The residents whose rates share a spreadsheet with a building that never sleeps? Illinois, like every state racing to host the boom, is writing the answer in real time, one zoning variance at a time, mostly at meetings where the public comment period runs to three minutes.

On the walk back, a kid on a bike rides the length of the fence line, one hand on the bars, reading the keep-out sign at ten miles an hour. The hum does not change. Somewhere inside, a model is being trained that will, by the time he is grown, be described as primitive. The building will still be there. The deal his town made for it will still be there too, thirty years and three mayors long. That is the thing about the acceleration: it moves at light speed on your phone, and at the speed of concrete on your block.
