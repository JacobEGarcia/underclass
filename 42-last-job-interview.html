Work - Cover Story

The Last Job Interview

Entry-level used to mean the bottom of a ladder. Now the ladder is being winched up into the cloud, and the people standing at the bottom are being told the climb was optional anyway.

Words M. Okafor - Illustration The Underclass Art Desk - 12 min read

Darius is 23, has a computer science degree, a 3.6 GPA, and a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet has 412 rows. Each row is a job he applied to between January and August. Column F tracks outcomes, and column F is almost monotonous: 289 rejections, 108 silences, eleven screens, four interviews, two finals, zero offers. He keeps the spreadsheet because his advisor told him job hunting is a numbers game. What nobody told him is that the numbers on the other side of the table are not people anymore.

"I did a first round where I never talked to a human," he says, sitting in a Humboldt Park cafe with the posture of someone reciting a war story that got boring. "You record answers into a webcam. A model scores your word choice, your eye contact, how long you pause. I practiced smiling at my own face for a week. Then I got rejected by the same email address that sent the invitation." He got better at it. That is the grim part. He learned to perform competence for a scoring function, and the function still said no.

Darius is not a cautionary tale about a lazy generation. He is an early data point in a reordering that almost nobody in charge wants to describe plainly: the entry-level job, the bottom rung where every career used to start, is the exact work the new machines do best.

The bottom rung of the ladder was not removed. It was automated.

THE WORK THAT USED TO TEACH YOU THE WORK

Every profession has a substrate of beginner labor. Junior developers write the boring code. Junior analysts build the decks. Paralegals do the document review. Reporters cover the city council meeting nobody reads about. The work is drudgery and that is the point: it is how a novice becomes trustworthy. It is also, item for item, the work that large language models and their agent offspring absorbed first, because it is structured, text-based, and forgiving of the machines' particular style of confident error.

The result is a quiet inversion. Companies still want seniors, people with judgment, people who have seen systems fail at 3 a.m. But judgment is made of junior work. A hiring manager at a logistics software firm in the West Loop, who asked not to be named because her employer is, in her words, "mid-figuring-this-out," put it plainly: "We need people with five years of experience. We are no longer sure where anyone is supposed to get the five years."

She runs first screens with an AI interviewer and says the tool is not the villain candidates imagine. "It screens out fewer people than the old resume keyword filter did. The difference is the pipeline behind it. We posted a junior role in March and got two thousand applications in four days, half of them written by agents, read by agents, ranked by agents." She laughs, not happily. "It is machines interviewing machines about a job a machine will mostly do."

THE ADVICE INDUSTRY

Into this gap has rushed an entire economy of advice. Learn to prompt. Become the person who manages the AI. Be a centaur. The advice is not wrong, exactly, but it shares a flaw: it describes a destination, not a route. Darius has heard all of it. He took a prompt engineering certificate course for $49. The course, he points out, was taught by an AI. "The final project feedback came back in eleven seconds," he says. "Nobody read it. I paid fifty bucks to be graded by the thing that took the job."

His parents, both nurses, ask why he does not just get a job at a hospital. He does not have a clean answer. The honest one is that hospitals are automating their own bottom rungs, intake coding and scheduling and transcription, and the rungs that remain, the human ones, require his parents' bodies: lifting, touching, bleeding-adjacent work. The economy's message to a 23-year-old in 2026 is strange and specific. The safe jobs are the ones that require a physical body, and the culture spent fifteen years telling bright kids to never plan on using theirs.

WHAT THE ROBOTS CANNOT SCHEDULE

There is a version of this story that ends in despair, and it is popular because despair photographs well. But the bottom of the ladder has been pulled up before. The typist pool, the switchboard, the drafting table, the darkroom. Each time, the rung vanished and people said the ladder was gone, and each time a new bottom appeared somewhere nobody was watching, usually in the messy layer between the machine's capability and the customer's actual life.

You can see the new layer forming if you squint. Small businesses drowning in tools they cannot configure. Clinics with AI systems nobody on staff can audit. A thousand companies with a model and no one who knows what to do with it on a Tuesday. The work is there. It is unglamorous, ill-defined, and badly titled, which is to say it looks exactly like entry-level work always has, right before an industry gives it a name.

Darius, for his part, stopped applying to the 412-row version of the future. He now does AI setup for three local businesses, a dentist, a property manager, and a restaurant group, found through his aunt's church. None of them posted the job. All of them pay him. "It's not a career," he says. "But it's a rung. I think I had to stop looking for the ladder and start nailing boards to the wall."

That may be the real story of the automation age's first decade: not that the ladder disappeared, but that it was privatized. The people who get to climb will be the ones who can build their own first steps, and that is a skill school never taught, because school never had to. Whether that selects for hustle or merely for luck is the question the next issue of this magazine will probably still be asking.
